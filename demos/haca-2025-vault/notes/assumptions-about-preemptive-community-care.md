# Assumptions about preemptive community care

A key accepted truth in health policy is that shifting care to communities enables more preemptive healthcare, which is believed to slow or stop disease progression, avoid future ill health, and better manage long-term conditions to prevent acute exacerbations. However, this assumption faces uncertainty. Moving some hospital facilities and staff, such as diagnostic and elective procedures, to non-hospital settings may lead to duplication of services, as some functions still require hospital settings. Furthermore, 'supply-induced demand' is a recognized factor in healthcare; building new facilities often leads to increased usage. Consequently, the shift to preemptive care might result in a net increase, rather than a reduction, in healthcare intervention and consumption.

The assumption that preemptive testing, screening, and genomic mapping will lower future healthcare demand by spotting disease early is untested and warrants caution. There is a significant risk that expanding such programs could drive up demand and divert resources away from patients with immediate needs. While methods exist to model the cost-effectiveness of screening programs, there is a strong commercial interest in expanding testing as a growth area. Policymakers are urged to resist reshaping health services to suit life sciences interests, ensuring that public health and patient interests remain central rather than being driven by commercial temptations.
