# Hospital Demand Drivers: Demographic and Non-Demographic

Hospital activity projections consider three main categories of change to forecast future demand. The first is demographic change, such as population growth and ageing, which typically increases activity. The second is non-demographic change, a category described as poorly named but encompassing everything else, including new medical technologies, improved patient expectations, and the general increase in healthcare activity. The third category involves mitigating activities or 'left shift', which includes factors that might reduce hospital demand, such as reduced smoking rates or better care pathways for frail and elderly patients to prevent falls.

Healthcare consumption in England is rising significantly faster than can be explained by demographic changes alone. While an aging population contributes to higher demand, actual spending exceeds projections based on age structure by £31 billion. This suggests that factors beyond demographics are driving increased consumption. These factors likely include higher levels of disease acquired as people live longer, but also technological advances and the associated rising expectations for new treatments and diagnoses. This phenomenon of demand rising at least twice as fast as demographic trends is observed across comparable countries, though the exact separate components of this increase are not fully understood.

Analysis by the Health Foundation and the Nuffield Trust compared actual healthcare activity growth against expected growth based on demographic changes over a 15-year period leading up to the pandemic. Elective procedures showed the largest discrepancy, with an average annual growth rate of 9.6%, compared to an expected growth rate of approximately 1% if activity had simply risen in line with demographics. In contrast, emergency admissions showed a much more modest gap, with actual growth around 1.9% versus an expected growth of just under 1%. To better understand this excess demand, further investigation is needed into the components of non-demographic pressures, including micro-analysis of specific health economies and examining the expectations of specific cohorts, such as 75-79 year olds.

To address the limitation that models only reflect the quality of their inputs, the project incorporates expert elicitation. Experts, such as those from the strategy unit, provide their beliefs about future scenarios. These expert opinions are fed into the model to generate precise statistical projections based on those beliefs. This approach helps hospitals understand potential futures, particularly for localized issues like frail care pathways, which central civil servants may not fully understand. The model acts as a reliable statistical engine that translates expert beliefs into quantifiable outcomes.
