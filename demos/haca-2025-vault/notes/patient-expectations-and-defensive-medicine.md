# Patient expectations and defensive medicine

There is a growing trend of risk aversion in hospitals and A&E departments, leading to increased testing and defensive medicine. A BMJ article highlighted that tests which were not performed a decade ago are now routinely ordered to ensure safety. General Practitioners have noted that having tests is now a well-established part of human illness behavior. Patients often feel dissatisfied if they are sent home to 'wait and see,' viewing a prescription or a referral for further testing as a positive and necessary outcome of a primary care visit. This suggests that making diagnostics more convenient may increase, rather than reduce, demand for interventions.

The assumption that the public desires care closer to home and lower levels of intervention is challenged by evidence of high demand for diagnostic tests and hospital-based reassurance. While convenience is a factor, patients and clinicians may find hospital settings reassuring due to the presence of highly qualified medical staff and immediate access to resources. It is unclear if this reassurance can be replicated in community-based models. Furthermore, shifting services out of hospitals may conflict with the public's appetite for diagnostic certainty and the perceived safety of hospital environments. The cohort of people aged 75 to 79 represents the highest consumers of acute care and is the first generation born into the NHS, which may shape their expectations regarding the model of care they receive.
