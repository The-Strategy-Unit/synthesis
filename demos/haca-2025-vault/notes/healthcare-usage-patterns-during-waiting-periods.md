# Healthcare usage patterns during waiting periods

Contrary to expectations, healthcare usage rates were generally higher prior to referral than during the waiting period or after treatment. This trend was most noticeable for A&E attendances. However, elective admissions showed a different pattern, with higher costs and usage rates observed while patients were waiting, followed by a decrease after treatment. These unexpected usage patterns challenged the assumptions required for causal analysis, specifically the hypothesis that longer waits would lead to a steady rise in activity during the waiting period and a decline post-treatment.

Analysis of healthcare utilisation before, during, and after elective waiting periods reveals distinct patterns. Usage is generally lower during the wait itself compared to the periods before and after. Increased use before joining the list may reflect efforts to secure referrals, while higher use after treatment may indicate follow-up care or complications. General practitioner (GP) appointments substantially outweigh Accident and Emergency (A&E) attendances in volume and cost-weighted terms, despite A&E being five to ten times more expensive per contact.

Longer waiting times are associated with greater relative increases in healthcare use after treatment. In North West London, emergency care use decreased for patients who waited less than 18 weeks compared to their waiting period, but increased for those who waited longer. The relative increase in emergency care use rose as waiting lengths increased. Although shorter-waiting groups, often older and frailer, have higher absolute healthcare use, the relative change from waiting to post-treatment periods shows a distinct trend for long waiters. This suggests that prolonged waiting may lead to increased complications or healthcare needs after treatment, warranting further causal analysis.

The observed patterns suggest that the relationship between waiting times and healthcare usage is complex and may not follow a simple linear trajectory. The higher usage prior to referral may indicate that patients are seeking care for symptoms that eventually lead to elective referral, while the decrease after treatment reflects the resolution of the condition. These findings highlight the need for careful consideration of baseline usage patterns when evaluating the impact of waiting times.
