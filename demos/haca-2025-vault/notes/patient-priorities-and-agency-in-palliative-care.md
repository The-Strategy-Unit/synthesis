# Patient priorities and agency in palliative care

Choice is critical in palliative care because serious conditions such as cancer, end-stage heart failure, respiratory disease, and frailty significantly limit options and reduce independence. Asking patients what is important to them restores agency and ensures care aligns with their values. Without these conversations, providers cannot know what matters to the patient. Responses can be unexpected; for example, a patient may refuse hospital admission due to concerns about pet care. Identifying priorities allows for tailored support, even when clinical circumstances are complex.

Realistic care involves transitioning from curative pathways to maintaining quality of life towards the end of life. Shared decision-making is often viewed as a key mechanism for this approach, enabling patients to discuss what matters to them and consider the impact of interventions on quality of life, particularly when life expectancy is limited. However, the concept of 'realistic' care is complicated by stark inequalities in the level of support patients can expect post-procedure. For example, the support available following an orthopedic procedure varies significantly depending on a patient's home environment and social support network. This disparity means that what is realistic for one patient may not be for another, highlighting an underexplored area requiring further investigation into how social determinants affect the feasibility of care plans.

A significant barrier to realistic care is that many individuals in their last year of life do not recognize their prognosis, partly due to a lack of societal frameworks for discussing these issues. Data from the Nuffield Trust showed that between 2019 and 2023, people whose cause of death was cancer received an average of 1.7 outpatient appointments in their last month of life, up from 1.2 in 2019. It remains unclear whether these appointments represented a tragedy of over-treatment or a necessary means for patients to maintain hope.

Initiating advanced care planning discussions is often hindered by patient hesitation and clinician inexperience. Patients may feel tentative about raising the topic, fearing that suggesting end-of-life planning implies they are in their final year of life. Conversely, clinicians frequently report lacking the experience or confidence to start these conversations. Additionally, a perceived lack of continuity of care, particularly in community settings, reduces opportunities for general practitioners and other providers to explore patient wishes effectively. Cultural barriers also exist; staff recruited from overseas may hold views that conflict with standard palliative care practices, viewing end-of-life conversations as inappropriate. Significant education and training are required to address these gaps.
