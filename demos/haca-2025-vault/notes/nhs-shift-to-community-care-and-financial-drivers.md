# NHS shift to community care and financial drivers

A central plank of health policy in England, articulated in the NHS ten-year plan and pursued for over three decades, is the shift of care from acute hospitals into communities. This involves moving services to GP practices, community health centers, and patients' homes. The policy is driven by three main patient-centered assumptions: that care close to home is more convenient, particularly for those with long-term conditions; that it allows for more preemptive care to slow disease progression; and that it provides more appropriate care, such as avoiding emergency admissions for frail elderly patients after falls or facilitating realistic end-of-life care.

A significant financial driver underpins this shift. Health spending in England has risen by approximately 2.5% in real terms on average over the last 15 years. However, there is a £37 billion gap between actual spending and what spending would have been since 2011 had it increased in line with the long-term trend of the first 60 years of the NHS. The hope is that shifting care out of hospitals will reduce overall healthcare consumption and unit costs, thereby helping to fill this gap. Additionally, actual healthcare spending is £31 billion more than it would be if consumption had only increased in line with the aging population, specifically the growth in over-70s. This indicates that healthcare demand is rising at least twice as fast as demographic changes alone would predict, a trend seen across comparable countries.
