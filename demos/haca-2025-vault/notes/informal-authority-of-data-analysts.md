# Informal Authority of Data Analysts

Analysts, often at lower organizational bands than their stakeholders, possess informal authority derived from their expertise in data and methods. This authority allows them to influence decisions, such as determining appropriate modeling techniques with senior leaders like a Chief Medical Officer. It involves resisting inappropriate stakeholder demands, such as requests for specific chart types, and asserting that certain methods are unsuitable due to data quality or methodological constraints.

Building this agency requires leadership development and confidence in technical expertise. Stakeholder management involves mapping internal team confidence and external stakeholder maturity regarding analytical requests. Strategies include identifying whether stakeholders demand inappropriate descriptive analytics or are ready for predictive and forecasting work. For resistant stakeholders, influence is exerted through strategic networking and identifying respected individuals within their networks who can help shift attitudes toward appropriate analytical practices.

To earn trust and credibility among clinical and operational leaders, analytical teams should prioritize building relationships. A historical example from strategic health authorities involved aligning analytical teams with specific clinical areas, such as dementia programs. This alignment allowed analysts to understand services, learn about treatment plans, and engage with clinical experts. This approach benefited both parties: clinicians gained access to data expertise they might not have known was available, and analysts found their work more interesting and impactful. The role of the analyst is described as partly educational, involving guiding stakeholders through data rather than just providing analysis.

Analysts are well-placed to identify special cause variation using statistical process control methods. However, they are unlikely to understand the root causes of these variations due to their distance from the service. Identifying the causes and implications of special cause variation requires forging relationships with clinical and operational managers who are close to the service. Technical expertise is critical, but maximizing benefit requires investing in the 'people side' of things to interpret data in context. While AI offers opportunities in data analysis, it is not viewed as a replacement for human relationships or education in building data literacy. AI may assist with technical tasks, such as interpreting Statistical Process Control (SPC) charts to determine if targets are being met or if trends are improving or deteriorating. However, AI cannot explain the underlying causes of changes in data. Understanding the causes of variation requires conversation and relationships with people close to the service, such as clinical and operational managers. Therefore, AI cannot replace the human element required for meaningful insight and decision-making.
