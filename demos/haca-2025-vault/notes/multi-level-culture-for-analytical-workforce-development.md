# Multi-level culture for analytical workforce development

Developing a sustainable analytical workforce requires a cultural shift across four distinct levels: individual, team, organization, and system. This process involves attracting, developing, and retaining staff with a wide range of analytical disciplines, including advanced modeling, AI, machine learning, economics, and evaluation.

At the **individual level**, analysts should be driven to succeed, seek personal development, step outside their comfort zone, and network. Key attributes for these roles include curiosity, critical thinking, and the ability to communicate influence to managers and senior decision-makers. Effective development involves exposing work to a wider range of peers to invite criticism and challenge, helping analysts move beyond focusing solely on technical skills toward developing influence and impact. For those starting in data and analytics, prioritizing the right mindset over acquiring technical skills is advised, as technical abilities can be taught but the 'art of questioning' and analytical thinking are harder to instill. Professionals are urged to understand the organization, identify what keeps senior leaders awake at night, and align their skills to contribute to solving those specific problems. Learning to code is recommended, but not focusing on it beyond what is required for daily tasks, and choosing open-source languages over proprietary ones to keep future career options open. Personal professional development planning is proposed as an alternative to standard professional development, allowing analysts to pursue interests driven by personal passion.

At the **team level**, culture should prioritize growth and development over simple job allocation, encouraging stretching and challenging tasks even if failure is a risk. This environment supports the transition from isolated technical work to broader engagement. To retain talent, jobs must be purposeful and worthwhile, avoiding the underutilization of high-skilled analysts on routine tasks like performance reports or dashboards. Creating an environment for learning through formal training, varied opportunities, and peer review within a network helps create a critical mass of analysts who can learn from each other.

At the **organizational level**, skills should be collaborated across departments through apprenticeships, secondments, and modern IT practices that enhance rather than impinge on analytical ability. Addressing large strategic questions may require multidisciplinary analytical teams that include health economists, statistics experts, and systems thinking specialists.

At the **system level**, initiatives like the national competency framework, government statistical service badging, and continuous professional development (CPD) help define career paths. National development centres can help assemble multidisciplinary teams. These systemic structures allow analysts to remain technical experts rather than solely becoming managers, supporting long-term retention and expertise within the field. There is also noted tension among some statisticians regarding Artificial Intelligence, with a need to overcome resistance to AI to help others use technology correctly.
