# Demographic disparities in NHS waiting times

Analysis of the Discover dataset and elective waiting lists reveals significant disparities in NHS waiting times, driven primarily by clinical specialty and demographic factors. While the majority of patients waited 18 weeks or less, between 27% and 33% exceeded this threshold, missing the NHS target of 92% within 18 weeks. The distribution of waiting times is heavily tailed, with many patients waiting multiple years, making the mean sensitive to outliers; consequently, median waiting times are used for analysis.

Age-related disparities are prominent. Seventy-three per cent of patients aged 85 and over waited less than 18 weeks, compared to only 63% of those aged 18 to 24. Older age groups consistently exhibit much shorter median wait times than younger groups. This pattern is likely reflective of clinical prioritisation based on need, as older individuals, those with higher frailty, and those with multiple long-term conditions tend to have shorter waits.

Gender and ethnicity also influence waiting times. Men waited slightly longer than women. White patients had shorter median wait times than other ethnicities. Patients with Black, Asian, or mixed ethnic backgrounds generally experienced longer median waiting times than White patients. These ethnic disparities remained evident even when accounting for the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) distribution and age bands. Modelled expected waiting times based on local age distributions indicated that observed ethnic differences were greater than expected, suggesting structural factors beyond clinical need.

Deprivation-related inequalities persist. Patients in more deprived areas generally experienced longer median waiting times than those in affluent areas. However, earlier analysis found no major differences in waiting time proportions when analysing deprivation quintiles or other demographic variables in isolation, highlighting the complexity of these factors.

The most significant differences in waiting times occur across medical specialties and locations. Neurosurgery and neurology exhibit the highest average waiting lengths, with neurology waits particularly prolonged in North West London. Conversely, elderly medicine and mental health show the lowest waiting times. Gynaecology waits were notably high in West Yorkshire and Cheshire and Merseyside.

These data-driven findings contrast with lived experiences reported by patient advisory groups, who expressed concern that older patients and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds were being left behind or forgotten.
