# Opioid prescribing and discharge outcomes

Opioid administration is a critical factor influencing delayed discharges, primarily because opioids induce nausea, which suppresses appetite. Since eating is a mandatory criterion for discharge, nausea necessitates the administration of antiemetics and prolongs the patient's stay. An analysis using equianalgesic doses revealed significant variations in the impact of different opioids on discharge likelihood. Fentanyl, an intravenous option, showed no significant effect on increasing delayed discharges. In contrast, dihydrocodeine nearly tripled a patient's chance of being a delayed discharge.

Despite its negative impact, dihydrocodeine was the most frequently prescribed opioid, largely because it appeared at the top of the hospital's prescribing protocol drop-down menu. This highlights how interface design can inadvertently drive suboptimal clinical choices. The analysis also distinguished between intravenous and oral opioids, identifying morphine and oxycodone as oral options with varying probabilities of causing delays. A flatter probability curve relative to the number of equidoses received indicates a better drug option regarding discharge efficiency. Ensuring consistency between opioids prescribed in the operating theatre and those administered in the recovery room is another area of focus, although data gaps exist due to differing recording methods (paper in theatres versus electronic in recovery).
