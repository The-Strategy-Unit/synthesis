# NHS Health Check barriers and uptake in Birmingham

A mixed-methods study conducted by Birmingham City University and Birmingham City Council in March 2025 identified significant barriers to NHS Health Check uptake among ethnically diverse communities in Birmingham. The research found that low awareness and misinformation were the most pervasive obstacles, with many participants unaware the programme existed or uncertain of its benefits. Among those aware, misconceptions about the purpose and outcomes of the checks deterred participation. Systematic barriers included ineffective communication strategies, such as unclear invitation letters, and logistical challenges like inconvenient appointment times. Additionally, the absence of culturally sensitive advice and deep-seated historical mistrust of healthcare institutions emerged as critical factors undermining engagement.

Analysis of participant data revealed notable disparities in engagement across different ethnic groups. Uptake rates were notably higher among Chinese and Nigerian groups, whereas Arab and White British men showed lower participation rates. Gender distribution varied across groups, with some communities showing balanced representation while others, such as Chinese and Nigerian participants, had a higher proportion of female participants. Gender-specific barriers were also noted: men often faced logistical issues, such as unavailability when contacted, whereas women expressed a desire for more culturally sensitive provisions, such as female testers.
<!-- synthesis-claim:2856e20550e7fd027a811640ae5126eb2ba265aac99f5fdc56c48a6d52534f6e -->

These findings highlight the need for multifaceted interventions addressing both informational and structural barriers. The study suggests that generic, one-size-fits-all commissioning strategies are ineffective and that targeted, data-driven investment is required to address specific community needs. By identifying specific barriers and facilitators within diverse communities, the study informs local decision-makers to create interventions that are both evidence-based and culturally sensitive, thereby reducing health inequalities.
