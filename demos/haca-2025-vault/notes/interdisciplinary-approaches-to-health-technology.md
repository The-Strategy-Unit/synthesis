# Interdisciplinary Approaches to Health Technology

Morley argues that health technology often fails because it is disciplinarily siloed. Failures occur when technical development ignores ethics, when ethical design ignores practical clinical constraints, or when clinical utility ignores legal frameworks like data protection law. She positions herself as a bridge between academia, policy, and clinical practice to ensure recommendations are useful and account for real-world constraints. Academia is described as often isolated from the practical realities of healthcare systems and policy.
<!-- synthesis-claim:e692ea9f8568d58f1e752fa1cc0991cd812688b48cec1d27d8ac8f75e3361b06 -->

To counter the abstraction inherent in data modeling, inclusive design requires diversifying design teams beyond computer scientists to include social scientists, artists, clinicians, and patient experience managers. This provides varied lived and disciplinary perspectives. Furthermore, fostering workplace inclusivity that allows employees to bring their 'whole person' to work, including emotions and full identity, is deemed essential for designing services for full people and addressing intersecting legal, ethical, and clinical challenges. Morley cites Professor Trish Greenhalgh as an admired figure who successfully bridges clinical practice, high-level social science theorizing, and practical application.
