# Medical patient boarding and its systemic impacts

Medical patient boarding refers to the delay between the decision to admit a patient to an Emergency Department (ED) and their actual admission to a ward bed. Medical patients, defined as those admitted to general internal medical or geriatric medical specialties, constitute approximately two-thirds of all ED admissions but account for just over 80% of all ED boarding time. This disproportionate wait time indicates that medical patients experience longer delays than other cohorts. Boarding often occurs in inferior environments, such as corridors or escalation areas, diverting resources from other patients requiring emergency assistance.

The impacts of boarding are categorized into downstream and upstream effects. Downstream impacts, which directly affect the boarding patients, include 30-day mortality, 30-day readmission rates, and medical length of stay. A key finding is the 'doubling effect' on length of stay: for every four hours of excess boarding time, patients spend slightly more than double that duration in acute care. For instance, 12 hours of boarding effectively accounts for an entire extra bed day, representing significant failure demand. The number needed to harm (NNH) for mortality is approximately 70; for every 70 patients delayed by more than four hours, there is one expected excess death.

Upstream impacts affect the broader system, including ED waiting times, ambulance handover times, and ambulance response times. These are causally linked to the level of the boarding queue rather than individual patient delays. Analysis reveals that while Category 1 ambulance responses (most critical) remain largely unaffected due to effective prioritization, Category 2 responses (serious instances like strokes) see significant increases in response times as boarding queues grow. This creates a 'cycle of harm,' a negative feedback loop where extended acute stays cause further downstream delays, exacerbating ED boarding and resource constraints.
